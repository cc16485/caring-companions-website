Why it matters

Independence is usually lost in small steps.

It rarely ends with one big event. Meals get skipped, the stairs feel harder, friends stop calling, a fall happens when nobody is around. Each of those is something a caregiver can help with, well before more care is needed.

How Wellness Care helps

Six things a caregiver does that add up to more good years at home.

Every visit is built around what keeps your loved one strong, sharp and connected, not just a list of chores.

A mind that stays engaged

Conversation, games, stories, hobbies

Real company, not a check-in from the doorway. Talking, laughing and doing things together is one of the best things for memory and mood.

Steady on their feet

Walks, gentle movement, getting out

Staying active keeps muscles and balance strong. We make moving part of the visit, at whatever pace feels right.

Eating well

Meal planning, cooking, grocery runs

When cooking for one gets tiring, meals get skipped. We cook their favorites together and leave food ready for later.

A safer home

Light housekeeping, laundry, clear paths

Clutter and piled-up laundry are trip hazards. A tidy home is a home with fewer falls.

Days that run smoothly

Medication reminders, routines, appointments

A friendly reminder to take medicine, keep the doctor's appointment, and stick to the routine that keeps them well.

Someone who notices

The same familiar caregiver

A caregiver who knows them notices small changes early, like less appetite or a new unsteadiness, so you can act before it becomes a crisis.

The little things

And help with the things that pile up.

Wellness Care also covers the small jobs that get harder to keep up with, and the ones that make a week feel like their own.

Phones, tablets and video calls

Help with the phone, the TV remote or a tablet, and setting up video calls so they can see the grandkids.

Pets and plants

Feeding and walking the dog, cleaning the litter box, watering plants and tidying the porch or patio.

Mail and paperwork

Sorting the mail, reading letters aloud, reminders for bills and help with simple forms. We don't handle money or banking.

Memories and hobbies

Photo albums, memory books, crafts, cards and letters to family, and decorating for the holidays.

A real visit

What four hours can look like.

Every plan is different. Here's one example of a Wellness Care visit for a mom who lives alone.

10:00Coffee together and a catch-up. How did she sleep? Anything on her mind?
10:30A load of laundry, a quick tidy of the kitchen, and the walkway cleared.
11:15Drive to the senior center for lunch with friends and a round of cards.
1:15A stop at the pharmacy and the grocery store on the way home.
1:45Put groceries away, cook a meal for tonight, and a reminder for her afternoon medicine.
2:00See you Thursday. The caregiver writes a short note about the visit.

Who it's for

A good fit if they are:

✓  Living alone and spending a lot of the day by themselves

✓  No longer driving, or not comfortable driving far

✓  Skipping meals, or cooking less than they used to

✓  Getting behind on housework or laundry

✓  Home from the hospital and getting their strength back

✓  Fine on their own, but you live far away and worry

Wellness Care does not include hands-on help like bathing or dressing. If that's needed, Personal Care is the next level.

Start before you have to

The best time to start is while things are going well.

Many families wait until after a fall or a hospital stay. Starting with a couple of visits a week while your loved one is doing well means they get to know their caregiver on good days, routines are already in place, and if more help is ever needed, it comes from someone they already trust.

It also gives you peace of mind now. You'll know someone is seeing them, eating with them and getting them out, even on the days you can't be there.
