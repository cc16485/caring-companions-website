For families hiring a caregiver directly

We would rather you do this well than do it with us.

Plenty of families hire a caregiver on their own. It is often cheaper, and when it works it works beautifully. It also goes wrong in ways nobody warns you about, usually around insurance, taxes, and what happens the first time your caregiver is sick.

So we put everything we know into one place and we are giving it away. No cost, no catch, and no obligation to ever hire us.

We are not being cute about that. If the right answer for your family is hiring somebody yourself, we want you to hire well.

Where to start →

Why an agency would tell you this

Fair question. Three honest reasons.

The first is that a family who hires badly usually ends up in a crisis, and crises are what put people in nursing homes. We would rather help you avoid that than pick up the pieces.

The second is that a lot of families who hire privately eventually need something we do have: a backup when their caregiver goes on vacation, a nurse to look at a wound, somebody overnight after a hospital stay. We would like to be the people you already trust when that day comes.

The third is simpler. We live here. The families in this town talk to each other, and we would rather be known as the agency that told people the truth.

If you take nothing else from this page: the person you hire is almost certainly your employee, not a contractor, and your homeowner's insurance probably will not cover them if they get hurt in the house.

What you are taking on, honestly

None of this is meant to scare you off. Every one of these has a straightforward answer, and most families handle them fine once they know the questions exist. The trouble only comes from not knowing.

1

You become an employer, not a customer

When you set someone's hours, tell them what to do, and provide the place they do it, the IRS almost always treats that person as your household employee, not an independent contractor. Handing them a 1099 at the end of the year is the single most common mistake families make, and it is the one that costs the most to unwind.

That means a W-2, and it means Social Security and Medicare taxes once you pay above a threshold the IRS sets annually. Federal unemployment tax kicks in at a lower amount. A payroll service built for household employers handles all of it for less than most people expect, and a CPA can tell you the current year's numbers in one phone call.

2

Workers' compensation, which is the one that can really hurt

A caregiver lifts your mother, hurts their back, and cannot work for four months. Without workers' compensation, that becomes a claim against you personally, and most homeowner's policies exclude household employees or cap it so low it does not matter.

Call your insurance agent before the first shift, not after. Ask them directly whether a paid caregiver working in the home is covered, and get the answer in writing. Some carriers will add it. Some will not, and you need to know which yours is.

3

Overtime rules that surprise people

If your caregiver is doing real personal care rather than just keeping company, federal overtime rules generally apply over forty hours in a week. Families who agree a flat weekly rate for a lot of hours can find themselves owing back wages later.

Agree an hourly rate and write down how overtime is handled before anybody starts. It is a five-minute conversation at the beginning and a miserable one afterwards.

4

The background check you can actually run

Missouri has the Family Care Safety Registry, and it is open to the public. Anyone can search it for a worker who has consented, and it covers the child and elder abuse registries, the employee disqualification list, and criminal history. It is run by the state health department at health.mo.gov/safety/fcsr.

Run it. Then run a separate criminal background check through a screening company, check the sex offender registry, and verify their driver's licence and auto insurance if they will ever drive your parent anywhere. And call every reference, including the ones they did not offer.

5

Nobody comes when they call in sick

This is the one families underestimate most. One caregiver means one point of failure. When they have the flu, a funeral, or a sick child of their own, the shift is yours, at whatever notice they can give you.

Have a plan before you need it. A second caregiver you have already met and vetted, a family member who can cover, or an agency you have already spoken to. Making that arrangement at 6am on a Tuesday is how good plans fall apart.

6

Money, medications, and no second set of eyes

Nothing here assumes bad intent. Most caregivers are wonderful and the great majority of families never have a problem. But an agency has supervisors dropping in, notes being read by somebody else, and a licence at risk. A private hire has none of that, so you have to build a little of it yourself.

Keep the chequebook and the card somewhere the caregiver does not access. Count medications on a schedule. Write down what you expect, in writing, so there is never a difference of memory about what was agreed.

When an agency is genuinely the better answer

Not always. But sometimes clearly, and you deserve to hear where the line is rather than work it out the hard way.

Hiring privately tends to work when

  • The hours are steady and predictable
  • One person can cover what is needed
  • Somebody in the family has time to manage it
  • You already have somebody in mind, often through church or a neighbour
  • The care is mostly company, meals, and light help
  • You are comfortable being an employer

An agency usually wins when

  • You need coverage that cannot fail, including nights and weekends
  • More than one caregiver is required
  • There is real personal care, transfers, or a fall risk
  • Dementia is advanced enough that behaviour is unpredictable
  • Nobody in the family has the bandwidth to manage a person
  • You want the insurance, the supervision, and the payroll to be somebody else's problem

Questions we get asked

Is this actually free, or is it a way to get me on the phone?
It is free. We will not put you on a list, and if you tell us you are hiring privately we will help you hire privately. The reason we do it is on this page: families who hire well stay home longer, and we would rather be the agency that helped than the one that sulked.
What should I pay a caregiver in Springfield?
It moves, and it depends on the hours and the level of care, so call and ask rather than trusting a number on a page. What we will say generally: paying under the local going rate is the most reliable way to lose a good caregiver in the first few months, and replacing somebody costs more than paying them properly did.
Can I just pay cash?
People do, and we are not going to lecture you. But understand what you give up: no workers' compensation, no unemployment protection for them, and personal liability if the arrangement is ever examined. It also means your caregiver builds no Social Security credit from years of real work, which matters to them more than most families realise.
Do you have caregivers you would recommend?
We cannot hand you our own staff, which we hope makes sense. What we can do is tell you honestly whether somebody you are considering raises any flags we would act on, and walk you through the reference calls.
What if it does not work out?
Call us. Genuinely. Whether that means covering a gap for two weeks while you find somebody else, or picking the whole thing up, we will pick up the phone. We would rather that than have you white-knuckle it because you feel awkward about asking.
We are outside Springfield. Can you still help?
Everything on this page works anywhere. HomeTogether Hire is nationwide, the templates apply wherever you are, and the Missouri registry covers the whole state. Our own caregivers only reach the Springfield area, so beyond that we are advice rather than staff.
