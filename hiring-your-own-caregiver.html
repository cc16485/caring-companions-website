For families hiring a caregiver directly

We would rather you do this well than do it with us.

Plenty of families hire a caregiver on their own. It is often cheaper, and when it works it works beautifully. It also goes wrong in ways nobody warns you about, usually around insurance, taxes, and what happens the first time your caregiver is sick.

So we put everything we know into one place and we are giving it away. No cost, no catch, and no obligation to ever hire us.

We are not being cute about that. If the right answer for your family is hiring somebody yourself, we want you to hire well.

Why an agency would tell you this

Fair question. Three honest reasons.

The first is that a family who hires badly usually ends up in a crisis, and crises are what put people in nursing homes. We would rather help you avoid that than pick up the pieces.

The second is that a lot of families who hire privately eventually need something we do have: a backup when their caregiver goes on vacation, a nurse assessment when care needs change, somebody overnight after a hospital stay. We would like to be the people you already trust when that day comes.

The third is simpler. We live here. The families in this town talk to each other, and we would rather be known as the agency that told people the truth.

If you take nothing else from this page: if you hire a caregiver directly and control how and when they work, they will usually be considered your household employee rather than an independent contractor. And do not assume your homeowner's insurance covers them if they are hurt in your home.

There are three ways to do this, not one

Most families arrive thinking the choice is agency or nothing, or private or nothing. It is worth ten seconds to see all three side by side, because the middle one is the one almost nobody knows about and it is the one that can pay for the care.

Before you pay privately, check whether Missouri Medicaid could pay for this.

This is the part families almost never hear about, and it is worth a phone call before you spend a dollar of your own money.

Missouri has a program called Consumer Directed Services. If the person needing help has Missouri Medicaid and a physical disability, and is able to direct their own care or has someone who can do it with them, they may qualify to choose and manage their own attendant while Medicaid pays for the authorized hours.

  • You choose the person, not an agency
  • It can be a family member, though not a spouse or legal guardian
  • A CDS provider runs payroll and handles the program requirements
  • The care is paid for by Medicaid rather than out of your pocket

Caring Companions Community Directed Services is a CDS provider. We can tell you in one conversation whether it is worth pursuing, and walk you through it if it is.

What you are taking on, honestly

None of this is meant to scare you off. Every one of these has a straightforward answer, and most families handle them fine once they know the questions exist. The trouble only comes from not knowing.

1

You become an employer, not a customer

The IRS looks at control. If you decide not only what work gets done but how and when it gets done, a caregiver you hire directly will usually be treated as your household employee rather than an independent contractor. Handing someone a 1099 at the end of the year is the most common mistake families make here, and the most expensive one to unwind.

Being a household employer can mean:

  • Getting an EIN from the IRS, which is free and takes minutes online
  • Completing Form I-9 to verify they can work in the United States
  • Having them complete a W-4, and issuing them a W-2 at year end
  • Withholding and paying Social Security and Medicare once wages pass the annual threshold the IRS sets
  • Working out whether federal and Missouri unemployment taxes apply to you

You do not have to work this out alone, and most families should not try. A payroll service built for household employers handles nearly all of it for less than people expect, and a CPA can give you this year's numbers in one phone call. IRS Publication 926 is the plain source if you want to read it yourself.

2

Insurance, which is the one that can really hurt

A caregiver lifts your mother, hurts their back, and cannot work for four months. Do not assume your homeowner's insurance protects you. Coverage for household employees varies a great deal between policies, and whether Missouri workers' compensation requirements apply can depend on your particular situation.

So before the first shift, not after, call your insurance agent. Tell them plainly that you are directly employing an in-home caregiver, ask exactly what coverage you have and what you need, and get the answer in writing. Some carriers will add coverage. Some will not, and you need to know which yours is while you still have choices.

3

Overtime rules that surprise people

If your caregiver is doing real personal care rather than just keeping company, federal overtime rules generally apply over forty hours in a week. Families who agree a flat weekly rate for a lot of hours can find themselves owing back wages later.

Agree an hourly rate and write down how overtime is handled before anybody starts. It is a five-minute conversation at the beginning and a miserable one afterwards.

4

The background check you can actually run

Missouri has the Family Care Safety Registry, and it is open to the public. Anyone can search it for a worker who has consented, and it covers the child and elder abuse registries, the employee disqualification list, and criminal history. It is run by the state health department at health.mo.gov/safety/fcsr.

Run it. Then run a separate criminal background check through a screening company, check the sex offender registry, and verify their driver's license and auto insurance if they will ever drive your parent anywhere. And call every reference, including the ones they did not offer.

5

Nobody comes when they call in sick

This is the one families underestimate most. One caregiver means one point of failure. When they have the flu, a funeral, or a sick child of their own, the shift is yours, at whatever notice they can give you.

Have a plan before you need it. A second caregiver you have already met and vetted, a family member who can cover, or an agency you have already spoken to. Making that arrangement at 6am on a Tuesday is how good plans fall apart.

6

Money, medications, and no second set of eyes

Nothing here assumes bad intent. Most caregivers are wonderful and the great majority of families never have a problem. But an agency has supervisors dropping in, notes being read by somebody else, and a license at risk. A private hire has none of that, so you have to build a little of it yourself.

Keep the checkbook and the card somewhere the caregiver does not access. Count medications on a schedule. Write down what you expect, in writing, so there is never a difference of memory about what was agreed.

When an agency is genuinely the better answer

Not always. But sometimes clearly, and you deserve to hear where the line is rather than work it out the hard way.

Private hiring tends to stay simple when

  • The hours are steady and predictable
  • One person can cover what is needed
  • Somebody in the family has time to manage it
  • You already have somebody in mind, often through church or a neighbor
  • The care is mostly company, meals, and light help
  • You are comfortable being an employer

Private hiring gets harder to manage when

  • Hands-on personal care is a large part of the day
  • Transfers need real training or equipment
  • Fall risk is high
  • Dementia behavior has become unpredictable
  • A shift genuinely cannot go unfilled
  • More than one caregiver is needed to cover the week

None of that means a private caregiver cannot do the work. Plenty are excellent at all of it. It means the supervision, the training, the backup, and the risk all land on you, and that load grows faster than most families expect.

Questions we get asked

Is this actually free, or is it a way to get me on the phone?
It is free. We will not put you on a list, and if you tell us you are hiring privately we will help you hire privately. The reason we do it is on this page: families who hire well stay home longer, and we would rather be the agency that helped than the one that sulked.
What should I pay a caregiver in Springfield?
It moves, and it depends on the hours and the level of care, so call and ask rather than trusting a number on a page. What we will say generally: paying under the local going rate is the most reliable way to lose a good caregiver in the first few months, and replacing somebody costs more than paying them properly did.
Can I just pay them cash, under the table?
Those are two different questions. You can absolutely pay by cash, check, or direct deposit, whatever the two of you agree, and cash by itself is not a problem. What you should not do is treat the wages as off the books because you happened to pay in cash. If that person is your household employee, the same tax, wage, and recordkeeping rules apply no matter how the money changes hands.

The reason it matters to them, and families rarely think about this: wages that are never reported build no Social Security credit. Somebody can work for a family for eight years and have nothing to show for it when they retire.
Do you have caregivers you would recommend?
We cannot hand you our own staff, which we hope makes sense. What we can do is tell you honestly whether somebody you are considering raises any flags we would act on, and walk you through the reference calls.
What if it does not work out?
Call us. Genuinely. Whether that means covering a gap for two weeks while you find somebody else, or picking the whole thing up, we will pick up the phone. We would rather that than have you white-knuckle it because you feel awkward about asking.
We are outside Springfield. Can you still help?
Everything on this page works anywhere. HomeTogether Hire is nationwide, the templates apply wherever you are, and the Missouri registry covers the whole state. Our own caregivers only reach the Springfield area, so beyond that we are advice rather than staff.
