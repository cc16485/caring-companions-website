Chronic Conditions

Home Care for Someone with COPD

Written by the Caring Companions team in Springfield · Updated November 2026

Read the guideCall (417) 234-8494

If your mom or dad has COPD, you have probably watched simple things get harder. A shower leaves them winded. The walk to the mailbox needs a rest halfway. They start skipping things they love because they are tired before they begin.

The goal at home is to save their breath for the parts of the day that matter to them. This guide covers what helps, what a week of help from us can look like, and the signs that mean it is time to call.

What makes the day easier

Most of the work of COPD is pacing. A few changes make a real difference:

  • Sit down for anything that can be done sitting: dressing, grooming, folding laundry, even part of cooking.
  • A shower chair and a handheld shower head turn the hardest task of the day into a manageable one.
  • Rest before they get worn out, not after. Plan the busy part of the day for when they feel best, often late morning.
  • Keep the air clean. Smoke, strong scents and dust can all set off coughing.
  • Use inhalers and oxygen the way the doctor prescribed, every day, not only on bad days.
  • Ask the doctor for a written COPD action plan. It spells out what to do when breathing gets worse, so nobody has to guess.

If they use oxygen, the oxygen company will go over safety at home. Keep their number on the fridge.

What a week of help can look like

For example: Tuesday and Friday, 9 a.m. to 1 p.m.

  1. A shower with a shower chair, taken slowly with rest breaks
  2. Help getting dressed sitting down
  3. Breakfast, and a few easy meals made for the days in between
  4. Dusting, vacuuming, laundry and changing the bed
  5. A ride to a doctor visit or pulmonary rehab, or picking up groceries and prescriptions
  6. Reminders to use inhalers at the times the doctor set
  7. A note after the visit, so our office knows how the day went

This is an example. Every plan is built with your family at the assessment visit, and it can change as needs change.

What our caregivers do, and what they don’t

Our caregivers can

  • Help with bathing and dressing at a slower pace, with breaks
  • Cook, clean, do laundry and keep the house dust-free
  • Remind them when it is time for inhalers or other medicine
  • Drive them to appointments, pulmonary rehab and errands
  • Keep walkways clear, including around oxygen tubing
  • Notice when they seem more short of breath or are coughing more, and tell us

Our caregivers don’t

  • Set up, adjust or change oxygen, or swap tanks
  • Give medicine or breathing treatments
  • Check oxygen levels or other vital signs
  • Do any nursing task

Your oxygen company, the doctor and, if ordered, a home health nurse handle those. We work around them.

When to call

Call the doctor the same day

  • More short of breath than usual, or getting winded doing less
  • Coughing more, or mucus that changes color or gets thicker
  • A fever or other signs of an infection
  • Their usual treatment does not seem to be helping as much

Call 911

  • Struggling to catch their breath or to talk
  • Lips or fingernails turning blue or gray
  • Not mentally alert, very confused or hard to wake
  • A very fast heartbeat
  • The treatment in their action plan is not working

Always follow what their own doctor told you first. If a caregiver sees an emergency during a visit, they call 911 and then our office, and we call you.

What to tell us at the assessment visit

Jot these down before we come. It helps us build the right plan the first time.

  1. Their lung doctor and any recent hospital or ER visits
  2. Oxygen: how much, when, and the oxygen company’s name and number
  3. Their inhaler and medicine schedule, in writing if you have it
  4. Which tasks tire them out most: the shower, stairs, cooking
  5. Things that set off their breathing: smoke, perfume, cold air
  6. What they still love to do, so we can save their energy for it
